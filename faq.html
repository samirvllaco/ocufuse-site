The basics6 questions
What is Ocufuse?

An app for your eyes, free to start: two-minute eye games, guided eye exercises and a daily eye journal you can share with your eye doctor.

More detail

It runs in your browser on any laptop, tablet or phone, with an iPhone app on the way. It's a journal and practice app. It isn't intended to diagnose, treat or monitor any condition.

Is Ocufuse free?

Ocufuse has a free plan and an optional paid plan, Ocufuse Plus. The free plan keeps the daily log, eye photos, your doctors and 7 free games (Zigzag jumps, Smooth pursuit, Letter catch, Shade Hunt, Dot Chase, Edge Spot and Visual Memory), with no card needed.

More detail

Ocufuse Plus adds every game, your full history, all 24 guide modules and the Doctor Brief. It costs $7.99 a month or $49.99 a year, with a free trial, or $119.99 once. Cancel anytime.

Do I need an account to play?

No. The four browser games here need only your email.

More detail

You need a free account to save your daily log, photos and practice history, and to use them on any device.

Do I have to download anything?

No. Ocufuse runs in your browser on a laptop, tablet or phone. You can add it to your home screen so it opens like an app.

Does it work on my phone?

Yes. Ocufuse runs in any modern browser on your phone or computer. Log in with the same account anywhere and your data is there.

Do I need any equipment?

No. Most games need only your screen. A few routines suggest something simple, like warm water and a clean cloth, and each one tells you before you start.

Is it for me?6 questions
My eyes are just tired from screens. Is this for me?

Yes, that's where most people start. Take a two-minute game or a 20-20-20 break between work sessions and note how your eyes felt. If something changes later, you already have a record to show.

I already see an eye doctor. Why would I need this?

Visits are short and symptoms come and go. Ocufuse keeps a dated log, photos and practice history between visits, then turns it into a short summary you can show. Your doctor sees the days they couldn't see in the room.

I'm busy. How much time does it really take?

About 20 seconds to log, a minute for a photo, and two minutes for a game. Use as much or as little as fits your day.

Are the games just for fun?

They're short, playful screen breaks and practice for things like following a moving dot or spotting small details. They're not a vision test and don't measure your eyesight. Many people use them as a two-minute pause between work sessions.

I've tried apps like this and stopped. Why is this different?

There's nothing to set up and nothing to keep up with. A check-in takes about 20 seconds, the games are short enough for a coffee break, and a reminder only nudges you if you haven't opened it that day.

What if I miss a few days?

Nothing breaks. Your history waits for you, and you pick up where you left off.

What to expect5 questions
Can games improve my eyesight?

No. They're screen breaks and practice, not treatment.

More detail

The games don't measure or change your vision, and they don't replace glasses, patching or therapy your eye care professional prescribes. For any change in your eyesight, see an eye care professional.

Will Ocufuse fix my eyes?

No, and we won't pretend it will. Ocufuse helps you take screen breaks, follow the exercises your eye doctor gave you, and keep a clear record of how your eyes feel. Treatment decisions stay with your eye care professional.

Is this a vision test or a medical app?

No. Ocufuse helps with habits, screen breaks and keeping a record to show your eye doctor. It does not diagnose or treat anything. For any eye concerns, see an eye care professional.

Can I use Ocufuse for lazy eye or strabismus exercises?

If your eye care professional has given you exercises, Ocufuse gives you guided steps, timers and a practice log to show them. It doesn't diagnose or treat amblyopia or strabismus, and games don't replace glasses, patching or therapy your clinician prescribes.

Can I share records with my doctor?

Yes. The Doctor Brief turns your log into a summary to share.

More detail

Print it, save it as a PDF or download a spreadsheet, for the period you choose. Nothing reaches a doctor unless you share it yourself. The Doctor Brief is part of Ocufuse Plus.

More about Ocufuse6 questions
What is an eye photo journal?

An eye photo journal is a dated series of eye photos taken the same way each day, paired with short notes about what happened. Consistent lighting, distance and views make photos comparable over time. Notes about sleep, screens or eye drops give your eye doctor context for symptoms that come and go.

Does Ocufuse use my photos to train AI?

Ocufuse does not use your photos or journal entries to train AI or machine learning models. Your data is used only to run the features you use, such as your timeline, comparisons and Doctor Brief. Ocufuse also does not sell data, share it with data brokers, or use it for advertising.

Privacy policy

Which languages does Ocufuse support?

The Ocufuse website is in English, Albanian, Turkish, Spanish, Italian and Serbian/Bosnian, and so are the web app's menus and sign-in screens, with more of the app on the way. Switching language changes the interface only, so your eye photos, notes and journal entries stay exactly as you recorded them.

Can children use Ocufuse?

Ocufuse is for people aged 18 and over, and it does not knowingly collect data from anyone under 18. Parents managing a child's eye condition should follow their eye care team's advice on records and photos. If a child under 18 has created an account, contact Ocufuse and it will be deleted.

Terms of use

Is Ocufuse an eye tracker?

Ocufuse is not an eye tracker. Eye trackers are devices or software that follow where a person is looking, often for research or accessibility. Ocufuse does not track gaze or monitor health. It is a photo journal that stores the eye photos and notes you choose to record.

Where does Ocufuse store my data?

Ocufuse stores account data, eye photos and journal entries with its hosting provider, Supabase; the privacy policy lists where each provider is based. Photos sit in private storage that only your signed-in account can read. Ocufuse does not sell your data, use it for advertising, or use your photos or entries to train AI models.

Eye photos and appointments8 questions
How do I take a photo of my eye for my doctor?

Take eye photos in soft, even light with the flash off, at a steady distance of about 30 to 40 cm, with your head level. Use the same views each time, such as straight ahead, left, right and up. Ask your clinician whether they want extra views, such as looking down.

Full photo guide

Why do my eye photos look different every day?

Eye photos change mainly because of lighting, camera distance, head angle and automatic phone processing. Tiredness, time of day, screen use, alcohol and allergies can also change how eyes look. A series of photos taken the same way is more meaningful than comparing any two single photos.

Why photos look different

What should I bring to an eye doctor appointment?

Bring your glasses or contact lenses, a list of medications and eye drops, and notes on when symptoms happen and how long they last. For symptoms that come and go, dated photos taken the same way each time help your eye doctor see days you cannot show in person.

Preparing for your appointment

How do I share my Ocufuse journal with my eye doctor?

Ocufuse shares your journal through a Doctor Brief that you export yourself. Open Brief, choose the date range and what to include, then print it, send it as text, or export entries as CSV. Clinicians have no access to your account, and nothing leaves Ocufuse unless you export it.

How do I send eye photos for a telehealth appointment?

Send eye photos for a telehealth appointment the way your clinic asks, usually through its patient portal or a secure message. Take each photo in even light, flash off, at a steady distance, with the views your clinician requests. Ocufuse can export a Doctor Brief with dated photos and notes.

How to take consistent eye photos

Can I track eye redness or dry eye symptoms with photos?

Ocufuse lets you photograph your eyes the same way each day and note symptoms such as redness, dryness or irritation, along with screen time, sleep and eye drops. It does not measure redness or judge severity. Your eye doctor uses the record as context alongside their own examination.

Why photos look different day to day

Can an app diagnose eye problems?

Consumer phone apps cannot replace an eye examination. Apps that claim to diagnose or monitor a condition may be regulated as medical devices, and phone cameras are not calibrated instruments. Use apps to record and share information, and let an optometrist or ophthalmologist examine your eyes and make a diagnosis.

What eye symptoms need urgent medical help?

Sudden double vision, sudden loss or dimming of vision, a suddenly drooping eyelid, new flashes or a curtain across vision, a painful red eye, and any eye injury need urgent medical care. Call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department rather than taking or comparing photos first.

Eye exercises10 questions
Do eye exercises improve vision?

Eye exercises do not improve nearsightedness, farsightedness, astigmatism or age-related reading problems, according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology. The main exception is convergence insufficiency, where clinician-supervised exercises have good evidence. Glasses, contact lenses or surgery remain the standard ways to correct refractive error.

Eye exercises: what the research says

Can eye exercises fix nearsightedness?

Eye exercises cannot reverse nearsightedness (myopia). Myopia comes from the shape and length of the eye, which exercises do not change. The American Academy of Ophthalmology and major eye health organizations agree that glasses, contact lenses or refractive surgery correct it, and an eye doctor should manage any myopia control treatment.

Eye exercise myths

What are eye exercises used for?

Eye care professionals prescribe eye exercises mainly for convergence insufficiency and some focusing or eye teaming problems, and sometimes for recovery after surgery or certain injuries. Exercises are not a treatment for glaucoma, cataracts, macular degeneration or refractive error. Do exercises only as your eye care professional prescribes them.

Guide to eye exercises

What are pencil push-ups?

Pencil push-ups are a home eye exercise for convergence insufficiency. You hold a pencil at arm's length, focus on a letter, and slowly bring it toward your nose while keeping it single. Clinicians prescribe the number of repetitions. In the CITT trial, office-based therapy outperformed pencil push-ups alone.

Pencil push-ups and the Brock string

What is a Brock string?

A Brock string is a cord with colored beads that eye care professionals use to train and check eye teaming. Holding one end at your nose, you focus on each bead and notice whether the strings appear to cross there. It is commonly prescribed in orthoptic and vision therapy programs.

Pencil push-ups and the Brock string

Is palming good for your eyes?

Palming means covering closed eyes with warm palms to rest them. It can feel relaxing, but there is no evidence that palming or the related Bates method improves eyesight, according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology. Palming is not a substitute for glasses, contact lenses or eye treatment.

Eye exercise myths

Can eye exercises help with needing reading glasses?

Eye exercises do not reverse presbyopia, the age-related loss of close focusing that leads to reading glasses, according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology. Presbyopia comes from the eye's lens becoming less flexible with age. Reading glasses, multifocal lenses, contact lenses or surgery are the standard ways to manage it.

Eye exercise myths

What is the Bates method?

The Bates method is an eye relaxation program developed by ophthalmologist William Bates in the early 1900s that claims to cure refractive errors without glasses. Techniques include palming and sunning. The American Academy of Ophthalmology states there is no evidence that the Bates method improves eyesight.

The Bates method and palming

What is an orthoptist?

An orthoptist is an eye care professional who specialises in disorders of eye movement and alignment, such as strabismus, amblyopia and convergence insufficiency. Orthoptists assess how the eyes work together, prescribe and supervise eye exercises, and often work alongside ophthalmologists in hospital eye departments, including in the NHS.

How orthoptists treat convergence insufficiency

How can Ocufuse help with prescribed eye exercises?

Ocufuse provides a Practice log for exercises your eye care professional prescribes. You record which exercise you did and when, and the log appears in your Doctor Brief for your next review. Ocufuse does not prescribe, adjust or teach exercises, so follow your clinician's instructions.

Eye exercise log

Digital eye strain3 questions
What is the 20-20-20 rule?

The 20-20-20 rule means every 20 minutes of screen use, you look at something 20 feet (about 6 metres) away for 20 seconds. The American Optometric Association recommends it to reduce digital eye strain by relaxing the focusing muscles and encouraging normal blinking during long periods of close work.

The 20-20-20 rule and digital eye strain

Does the 20-20-20 rule work?

The 20-20-20 rule reduced digital eye strain and dry eye symptoms in a 2022 study of computer users who received regular break reminders. The benefit faded about one week after participants stopped taking breaks. Regular breaks help comfort but do not change eyesight or correct vision problems.

What the research found

What is digital eye strain?

Digital eye strain, also called computer vision syndrome, is a group of eye and vision symptoms from prolonged screen use. Common symptoms include eyestrain, headaches, blurred vision, dry eyes, and neck and shoulder pain. The American Optometric Association links risk to two or more hours of continuous screen use.

Digital eye strain explained

Vision therapy9 questions
What is vision therapy?

Vision therapy is a structured program of eye exercises and activities supervised by an optometrist or orthoptist to treat certain eye teaming and focusing problems. Sessions usually last 30 to 60 minutes once or twice a week, with exercises to practise at home between visits.

Does vision therapy work?

Vision therapy has strong evidence for convergence insufficiency. In the CITT trial of children aged 9 to 17, office-based therapy succeeded in 73 percent of cases versus 35 percent with placebo. Evidence for most other uses is limited, and ophthalmology groups advise against vision therapy for learning disabilities.

The CITT trial explained

Can vision therapy help dyslexia or learning disabilities?

Vision therapy does not treat dyslexia or learning disabilities, according to a joint statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Academy of Ophthalmology and pediatric ophthalmology groups. Children with reading difficulties should still have an eye exam, alongside educational assessment, to rule out treatable vision problems.

What is convergence insufficiency?

Convergence insufficiency is a condition where the eyes do not turn inward together easily for close work. It can cause eyestrain, headaches, double or blurred vision and losing your place when reading. Clinician-supervised vision therapy, often with home exercises, is the treatment with the strongest evidence.

Convergence insufficiency explained

Who provides vision therapy?

Optometrists and orthoptists provide vision therapy, often in specialist clinics, and ophthalmologists diagnose and manage the underlying eye conditions. For example, NHS orthoptists at Cambridge University Hospitals supervise home exercises for convergence insufficiency with reviews every one to two weeks. Ask your eye doctor about a referral.

Who is a good candidate for vision therapy?

Good candidates for vision therapy are people diagnosed with convergence insufficiency or certain focusing and eye teaming problems after a full eye examination. Cleveland Clinic notes the evidence mainly supports convergence insufficiency and accommodative dysfunction. Vision therapy is not recommended for learning disabilities or for correcting refractive error.

Convergence insufficiency explained

Is vision therapy covered by insurance?

Vision therapy coverage varies by country, insurer and plan, and often depends on the diagnosis. Some plans cover treatment for a diagnosed condition such as convergence insufficiency but not other uses. Ask the clinic for the diagnosis and treatment codes, then confirm coverage with your insurer before starting.

What is the difference between vision therapy and orthoptics?

Orthoptics is the assessment and non-surgical treatment of eye movement and alignment problems, usually by orthoptists, with evidence for convergence insufficiency. Behavioral vision therapy, offered by some optometrists, covers a broader range of goals. Ophthalmology and pediatric groups consider the evidence for many of those broader uses limited.

How long does vision therapy take?

Vision therapy programs commonly last several weeks to a few months, depending on the condition and progress. The CITT trial for convergence insufficiency used a 12-week program of weekly office sessions with home practice. Your eye care professional sets the schedule and reviews progress at regular visits.

Eye conditions explained3 questions
What is amblyopia?

Amblyopia, often called lazy eye, is reduced vision in one eye caused by abnormal visual development in childhood. The brain favours the other eye. Treatment is most effective in young children and can include glasses, patching the stronger eye, or atropine eye drops, prescribed and monitored by an eye care professional.

What is strabismus?

Strabismus is a misalignment of the eyes, where one eye turns in, out, up or down while the other looks straight ahead. It can be constant or come and go. Treatment depends on the cause and may include glasses, prism lenses, eye muscle surgery, or exercises for some types.

What is accommodative dysfunction?

Accommodative dysfunction is difficulty adjusting the eyes' focus between distances or sustaining focus up close. It can cause blurred near vision, eyestrain and headaches during reading. It is diagnosed during an eye examination, and treatment may include glasses for close work or exercises prescribed by an optometrist or orthoptist.
