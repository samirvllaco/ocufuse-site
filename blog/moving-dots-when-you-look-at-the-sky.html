Tiny moving dots when you look at the sky: what they are

The tiny bright dots that dart about against a blue sky are white blood cells. What they are and how they differ from floaters and flashes.

Explainers4 min read17 October 2026Ocufuse team
In short

The tiny bright dots that zip around when you look at a clear blue sky are your own white blood cells moving through vessels in front of the retina. The American Academy of Ophthalmology says seeing them is normal. They're different from floaters, and new floaters or flashes need prompt care.

Look up at a clear blue sky for a few seconds and you may see tiny bright dots darting around along wiggly paths. They seem to speed up and slow down, and they vanish almost as soon as you notice them. Many people have seen them since childhood without knowing what they were.

The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) explains that they're your own white blood cells.

How the eye works explains the retina, the layer these cells pass in front of, and the blind spot is another everyday part of seeing that most people never notice.

What you're seeing

Tiny blood vessels run across the surface of the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. Red blood cells absorb blue light. White blood cells let blue light through to the retina. So when a white blood cell travels through one of these vessels, a little more blue light gets through behind it, and you see a bright moving dot.

This effect has a name: the blue field entoptic phenomenon. "Entoptic" means the image comes from inside your own eye. The AAO adds some details:

  • The dots speed up and slow down with your pulse.
  • Some have a dark tail, which is a cluster of red blood cells following the white cell.
  • They can look like little worms.
  • They usually vanish within a second or so.

A clear blue sky is the ideal background because it's bright, even and rich in blue light. The AAO says seeing these dots is normal, though not everyone notices them.

How they differ from floaters and flashes

Floaters are clumps in the vitreous, the gel inside the eye. Our post on eye floaters explains them in more detail.

Flashes that come with migraine

Some people see a different effect: shimmering spots or jagged, zig-zag lines that spread across their vision. The AAO notes that flashes like these may be linked to migraine, can last up to an hour, and may happen with or without a headache. They're different from both sky dots and floaters. If you've never had them before, or they change, get them checked.

A word about looking at the sky

Look at open blue sky, never toward the sun. The AAO warns that looking at the sun can permanently damage the retina. See watching a solar eclipse safely.

When to see someone

The AAO says seeing dots against a large clear space such as the sky is not a concern. It also says to call an ophthalmologist right away if you notice:

  • A change in your vision
  • A sudden onset of flashes
  • A sudden onset of new floaters

The AAO notes that new or increased flashes or floaters can be a sign of a retinal detachment, which needs urgent care.

If you're unsure which you're seeing, write down what it looks like, when you see it, and whether it's new. That description is useful to an eye doctor, and our guide on describing vision changes has words that help.

Get help straight away ifyou notice a sudden increase in floaters, new flashes of light, or a shadow or curtain across your vision. See the warning signs that need help straight away.

Common questions

What are the moving dots I see in the sky?

The American Academy of Ophthalmology explains they're your own white blood cells moving through the small blood vessels in front of the retina. The effect is called the blue field entoptic phenomenon.

Is it normal to see dots when looking at the sky?

Yes. The American Academy of Ophthalmology says seeing them against a clear sky is normal, though not everyone notices them.

Are sky dots the same as floaters?

No. Sky dots are all about the same size, bright, and keep moving when your eye stops. Floaters vary in size, look like darker shadows, and drift and settle when your eye stops moving.

Why do the dots move in time with my pulse?

Because they're blood cells. The American Academy of Ophthalmology says they speed up and slow down with your pulse.

When should I worry about seeing spots?

The American Academy of Ophthalmology advises calling an ophthalmologist right away for a change in vision or a sudden onset of flashes or floaters.
