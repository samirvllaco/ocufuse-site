Why do eyes look red in flash photos?

The red-eye effect is light reflected from the back of the eye. Why it happens, why some photos show it, and when a reflection needs a check.

Explainers5 min read17 October 2026Ocufuse team

In short

Red eyes in flash photos happen because the flash lights up the blood-rich back of the eye faster than the pupil can narrow, and the light bounces straight back to the camera. Red in both eyes is usual. A white, yellow or black reflection, or one eye that repeatedly looks different, is worth showing to a doctor.

You take a photo at a party with the flash on, and everyone's pupils glow red. It's one of the oldest problems in photography, and it has a straightforward explanation: you're seeing light reflected from the back of the eye.

How the eye works explains the back of the eye, the part that reflects the flash, and whether eye colour can change covers other differences people notice in photos.

What's happening

Cleveland Clinic calls it the red reflex, or more accurately the fundus reflex: light reflecting off the inner back of the eye and showing through the pupil. The back of the eye has a rich blood supply, which is why the reflection looks red.

The timing matters. Cleveland Clinic explains that the flash reflects off the back of the eye faster than the pupils can narrow. In a dim room the pupils are already wide, so more light gets in and more comes back out.

Why some photos show it and others don't

  • Room light. Cleveland Clinic notes dim rooms make it more likely, because the pupils are wider.
  • Angle. It can be less obvious in one eye depending on the angle or the lighting.
  • Where the person is looking. The AAO notes a reflection can look different if someone is looking off to the side rather than at the lens.
  • Camera settings. Many cameras have a red-eye reduction setting. The AAO mentions it when explaining how to take photos of a child's eyes.

The colour also varies. Cleveland Clinic says the reflex can look reddish or orange in people with lighter skin, and yellow, green or bluish in people with darker skin.

Fewer red eyes in your photos

When a different reflection is worth checking

Red in both eyes, looking about the same, is the usual result. The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) says that when a child is looking at the lens and both eyes look red, that's usually a good sign that nothing is blocking the view to the back of either eye.

The AAO describes an abnormal reflex as a white, yellow or black reflection in one or both eyes, and says it can be a warning sign of an eye condition. It notes that the most common cause is a need for glasses, and that rarer causes can be serious. If only one eye looks red, or one is dimmer, that can be a sign the eyes aren't aligned.

Cleveland Clinic adds a sensible note: don't panic over a difference in a single picture. If you see the same thing again and again, contact the child's doctor and ask for an appointment. The check takes minutes and is painless.

Bring the photos with you. Our post on photographing your child's eyes for the eye doctor covers how to take useful ones, and consistent eye photos covers lighting and distance.

Photos are a prompt to see a professional, not a test. Ocufuse can store photos with dates, but it doesn't analyse them or judge whether a reflection is normal.

Get help straight away ifa child suddenly develops a turned eye, double vision, a drooping eyelid, an eye injury, or a red and painful eye. See the warning signs that need help straight away.

Common questions

Why do eyes look red in photos?

The flash lights up the blood-rich back of the eye faster than the pupil can narrow, and the light reflects back to the camera. Cleveland Clinic calls it the red reflex or fundus reflex.

How do I avoid red eyes in photos?

More room light narrows the pupils, so turning lights on or using daylight instead of a flash helps. Many cameras also have a red-eye reduction setting.

Is one red eye and one white eye in a photo a problem?

The American Academy of Ophthalmology describes a white, yellow or black reflection, or one eye looking different, as a possible warning sign worth showing to a doctor, especially if it appears in more than one photo.

Should I worry about one odd photo?

Cleveland Clinic says not to panic over a difference in a single picture. If you see the same thing again and again, contact the child's doctor. The check is quick and painless.
