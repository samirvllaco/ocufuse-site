A drooping eyelid: what to record and when it is urgent

When one eyelid sits lower than before, photos taken the same way over time are useful to your doctor. How to take them, what to note, and the signs that cannot wait.

Symptoms12 October 20265 min readBy the Ocufuse team
Start hereAn eyelid that droops suddenly, over minutes or hours, needs urgent medical help, especially with double vision, a headache, pupils of different sizes, weakness, numbness or difficulty speaking. Don't wait to take photos. Call emergency services or go to an emergency department. See the warning signs that need help straight away.

A drooping upper eyelid can develop slowly over months or years, and sometimes it varies through the day. Because it can be subtle, people often aren't sure whether it has changed. A consistent photo record and a few notes help answer that question for your doctor.

Find older photos

Doctors often find it helpful to compare with how your eyes looked in the past. Look through your camera roll or ID photos for clear, front-facing pictures from a few years ago. Note the approximate date of each.

Take new photos the same way each time

  • Same place, same soft light, no flash
  • Phone at eye height, about 30 to 40 cm away
  • Chin level, looking straight into the lens
  • Relax your forehead. Raising your eyebrows can lift the eyelids and hide a droop.
  • Add looking up, left and right, so you have the same four views each time

Ocufuse guides you through these four views with on-screen framing. Our guide to consistent eye photos explains why each step matters.

Notes that help

  • Time of day. Is it the same morning and evening, or does it change as the day goes on? Photos at both times are useful.
  • Tiredness. Does it change after reading, screen work or exercise?
  • Other symptoms. Double vision, blurred vision, eye pain, headaches, or trouble chewing, swallowing or speaking. Mention all of them, even if they seem unrelated. If you have trouble breathing or swallowing, get emergency help straight away.
  • Contact lenses and eye surgery. Note how long you have worn lenses and any past procedures.

What not to do

Don't try to measure or diagnose the droop from photos. Phone photos change with light, angle and distance, and only an examination can tell what is going on. Bring the photos as context and let your doctor interpret them.
